SPORTS NUTRITION BASICS — EVIDENCE NOTES FOR ADULT ENDURANCE RUNNERS

CARBOHYDRATE AND DAILY FUEL: Carbohydrate needs vary with training volume, intensity, and recovery time. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements summarizes a broad athlete range of about 3–10 g/kg/day; it is not a single target for every runner. Choose a level with a sports dietitian or based on a credible sport-nutrition plan. Source: NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, Dietary Supplements for Exercise and Athletic Performance, https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/exerciseandathleticperformance-healthprofessional/.

CARBOHYDRATE DURING LONG RUNS: For prolonged endurance exercise, sports-nutrition references commonly discuss about 30–60 g carbohydrate per hour, depending on duration, intensity, tolerance, and training. Practise a fueling plan during training; do not start a new product or large dose on race day. Source: American College of Sports Medicine position stand, Exercise and Fluid Replacement, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9303999/.

PRE-RUN FOOD: There is no single required pre-run food or timing that suits everyone. A familiar, carbohydrate-containing meal or snack can be chosen according to the time before the run, portion tolerance, duration, and personal preference. Keep choices that cause gastrointestinal symptoms out of the plan and practise race-day choices during training. Source: Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada, and American College of Sports Medicine joint position statement, Nutrition and Athletic Performance, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26891166/.

PROTEIN NEEDS: The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements reports a general athlete protein range of about 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day; needs depend on the person and training. This is a broad reference range, not an individualized prescription. Source: NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, Dietary Supplements for Exercise and Athletic Performance, https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/exerciseandathleticperformance-healthprofessional/.

PROTEIN AFTER EXERCISE: A source summarized by NIH reports about 0.3 g/kg of high-quality protein within 0–2 hours after exercise, then protein-containing meals or snacks every 3–5 hours as one strategy for training adaptation. Whole foods can contribute; supplements are not required for everyone. Source: NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, Dietary Supplements for Exercise and Athletic Performance, https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/exerciseandathleticperformance-healthprofessional/.

RECOVERY MEALS: A practical recovery meal can combine a carbohydrate food with a protein food, especially when another demanding session follows soon. Examples vary by culture and diet: rice or bread with beans, lentils, tofu, eggs, dairy, fish, or meat as appropriate. Total intake, appetite, tolerance, and time until the next session matter; there is no universal 30-minute deadline. Source: Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada, and American College of Sports Medicine joint position statement, Nutrition and Athletic Performance, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26891166/.

HYDRATION: Fluid needs differ with body size, weather, pace, duration, clothing, and individual sweat losses. Both too little and excessive fluid can be harmful; avoid forcing large amounts of water. For long or hot sessions, an individualized plan may help. Source: National Athletic Trainers' Association position statement, Fluid Replacement for the Physically Active, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28985128/.

VEGETARIAN AND VEGAN PATTERNS: Appropriately planned vegetarian and vegan diets can support sport. Include varied energy-dense foods and regular protein sources such as legumes, soy foods, grains, nuts, and seeds when tolerated. A fully plant-based pattern needs attention to nutrients that are harder to obtain from unfortified plant foods, including vitamin B12; individualized planning is appropriate. Source: Vegetarian diets: nutritional considerations for athletes, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16573356/; Nutritional Considerations for the Vegan Athlete, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37127187/.

SUPPLEMENTS: Evidence and safety vary by supplement, person, dose, sport, and product. Supplements can interact with medicines or contain undeclared ingredients. Food-first advice is appropriate; do not start, stop, or dose a supplement based on this chatbot. Ask a health professional or registered sports dietitian, especially if under 18, pregnant, taking medication, or managing a condition. Source: NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, Dietary Supplements for Exercise and Athletic Performance, https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/exerciseandathleticperformance-healthprofessional/.

MEDICAL CONDITIONS AND ALLERGIES: General sport-fueling information cannot determine safe foods for a person's allergy, coeliac disease, diabetes, kidney disease, pregnancy, eating disorder, or other medical condition. For allergies, check ingredients and cross-contact with the food provider; this chatbot cannot guarantee that a food is safe. Get personalized advice from a registered dietitian or clinician.

WEIGHT LOSS AND LOW ENERGY AVAILABILITY: This chatbot does not prescribe calorie deficits, rapid weight loss, or restrictive diets for athletes. Persistent fatigue, recurrent injuries, missed or irregular periods, or declining performance can warrant assessment by a qualified clinician or sports dietitian. Source: International Olympic Committee consensus statement on Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37752011/.
