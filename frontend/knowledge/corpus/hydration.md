## How much water to drink in a day
Source: EFSA Panel on Dietetic Products, Nutrition and Allergies. Scientific opinion on dietary reference values for water. EFSA Journal 2010;8(3):1459
Link: https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1459
Cite: EFSA 2010

The European Food Safety Authority sets adequate total water intake at about 2.5 litres a day for men and 2.0 litres a day for women in a mild climate. This includes the water in food, which gives about 20% of the total. Athletes need more: on top of the daily amount, they must replace what they lose in sweat during training. Hot and humid weather, as in much of India, raises sweat losses a lot.

## Drinking before training
Source: Sawka MN et al. American College of Sports Medicine position stand: exercise and fluid replacement. Med Sci Sports Exerc 2007;39(2):377-390
Link: https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31802ca597

Start training well hydrated. About 4 hours before exercise, drink about 5-7 ml per kg of body weight (for a 60 kg runner, about 300-420 ml). If you pass no urine or it is dark, drink another 3-5 ml per kg about 2 hours before. Starting early leaves time to pass extra urine before the session. A little salt in food or drink before exercise helps the body hold on to the water.

## Drinking during training and testing your sweat rate
Source: Sawka MN et al. American College of Sports Medicine position stand: exercise and fluid replacement. Med Sci Sports Exerc 2007;39(2):377-390
Link: https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31802ca597

Sweat rates vary from about 0.5 to more than 2 litres per hour between people and with the weather. The aim during exercise is to avoid losing more than 2% of body weight from water loss, because that starts to hurt endurance performance. You can measure your own sweat rate: weigh yourself before and after a 1-hour run (without clothes, after passing urine), add the water you drank, and the loss in kg is roughly your sweat loss in litres per hour. Do not drink so much that you gain weight during exercise.

## Salt and sports drinks for long or hot runs
Source: Sawka MN et al. American College of Sports Medicine position stand: exercise and fluid replacement. Med Sci Sports Exerc 2007;39(2):377-390
Link: https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31802ca597

Sweat contains salt (sodium), on average about 35 mmol per litre with a wide range between people. For long sessions, heavy sweaters and hot weather, the ACSM suggests drinks with about 20-30 mmol per litre of sodium and 5-10% carbohydrate. A homemade version: 1 litre of water with about 1/4 level teaspoon of salt (about 1.5 g) and 4-5 level tablespoons of sugar or glucose. Lemon or a little juice can be added for taste. Store-bought ORS also works for long hot runs. Salty food after the session (dal, sambar, curd rice with salt) helps replace losses.

## Drinking after training
Source: Thomas DT, Erdman KA, Burke LM. ACSM, AND and Dietitians of Canada joint position: Nutrition and Athletic Performance. Med Sci Sports Exerc 2016;48(3):543-568
Link: https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000852

To fully replace water lost in sweat, drink about 1.25-1.5 litres for every 1 kg of body weight lost during the session, spread over the next few hours rather than all at once. The extra 25-50% covers urine losses. Eating salty food or drinking a salty drink with it helps the body keep the water. When there is plenty of time before the next session, normal meals and drinks usually restore hydration.

## Danger of drinking too much water (hyponatraemia)
Source: Hew-Butler T et al. Statement of the Third International Exercise-Associated Hyponatremia Consensus Development Conference, Carlsbad, California, 2015. Clin J Sport Med 2015;25(4):303-320
Link: https://doi.org/10.1097/JSM.0000000000000221

Exercise-associated hyponatraemia means the blood salt (sodium) level falls too low. The main cause is drinking more water or sports drink than you sweat out, especially in slower runners in long events. Signs include headache, swelling of hands, nausea, vomiting, confusion and, in severe cases, fits. It can be fatal. The consensus advice is to drink according to thirst, and never to drink "as much as possible". Gaining weight during a race is a warning sign of overdrinking.

## Checking hydration with urine colour
Source: Armstrong LE et al. Urinary indices of hydration status. Int J Sport Nutr 1994;4(3):265-279
Link: https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsn.4.3.265

Urine colour is a simple daily check of hydration. Pale straw or light lemon coloured urine suggests good hydration; dark yellow or brown urine, or passing very little urine, suggests you need to drink more. Some vitamins (B vitamins) can turn urine bright yellow, and first-morning urine is darker, so use the check through the day. Feeling very thirsty is another sign to drink.
