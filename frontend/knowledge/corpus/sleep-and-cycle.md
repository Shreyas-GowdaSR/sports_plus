## How much sleep athletes need
Source: Hirshkowitz M et al. National Sleep Foundation's sleep time duration recommendations: methodology and results summary. Sleep Health 2015;1(1):40-43
Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2014.12.010

The US National Sleep Foundation recommends 7-9 hours of sleep a night for adults aged 18-64 and 8-10 hours for teenagers aged 14-17. Athletes in hard training often benefit from the upper end, and a short afternoon nap can help on heavy days. Keep a regular sleep and wake time, keep the room dark and cool, and avoid screens, heavy meals and caffeine late in the evening.

## More sleep can improve performance
Source: Mah CD, Mah KE, Kezirian EJ, Dement WC. The effects of sleep extension on the athletic performance of collegiate basketball players. Sleep 2011;34(7):943-950
Link: https://doi.org/10.5665/SLEEP.1132

In a Stanford study, college basketball players tried to sleep about 10 hours a night for 5-7 weeks. Their sprint times got faster, shooting accuracy improved by about 9%, and they reported better mood and less tiredness. The study was small, but it suggests many athletes are not sleeping as much as their bodies need, and that more sleep is a simple, free way to recover better.

## Short sleep and injury risk
Source: Milewski MD et al. Chronic lack of sleep is associated with increased sports injuries in adolescent athletes. J Pediatr Orthop 2014;34(2):129-133
Link: https://doi.org/10.1097/BPO.0000000000000151

In a study of young athletes, those who usually slept less than 8 hours a night were about 1.7 times more likely to get injured than those who slept 8 hours or more. Poor sleep also slows reaction time and recovery and raises the chance of getting ill. When training gets harder, protecting sleep is as important as food.

## Training and the menstrual cycle
Source: McNulty KL et al. The effects of menstrual cycle phase on exercise performance in eumenorrheic women: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Sports Med 2020;50:1813-1827
Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01319-3

A review of 78 studies found that performance may be slightly lower in the early part of the cycle (around the period), but the effect is small and differs a lot between women. There is no need to stop training during periods. A better approach is to track your own cycle and symptoms (cramps, tiredness, mood, sleep) and adjust training on days you feel poor. Periods that stop, become very irregular or very heavy are not normal for athletes and need a doctor's check, as they can signal under-fuelling or low iron.
