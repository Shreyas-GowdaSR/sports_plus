## How common running injuries are
Source: Videbaek S, Bueno AM, Nielsen RO, Rasmussen S. Incidence of running-related injuries per 1000 h of running in different types of runners: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Sports Med 2015;45(7):1017-1026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0333-8

This review found that new (novice) runners get about 17.8 injuries per 1000 hours of running, while regular recreational runners get about 7.7 per 1000 hours. Beginners are more than twice as likely to get injured for the same running time, mostly because bones, tendons and muscles have not yet adapted. Beginners should build up slowly, and use walk-run sessions at first.

## How fast to increase weekly running
Source: Nielsen RO et al. Excessive progression in weekly running distance and risk of running-related injuries: an association which varies according to type of injury. J Orthop Sports Phys Ther 2014;44(10):739-747
Link: https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2014.5164

In this study of new runners, those who increased their weekly distance by more than about 30% over two weeks had a higher risk of some injuries, such as runner's knee, ITB syndrome, shin pain and hip and hamstring injuries. A separate trial (Buist 2008) found that following the strict "10% rule" did not lower injury compared with a normal programme. The message: avoid big jumps in weekly distance, build gradually, and include easier weeks every 3-4 weeks.

## Strength training prevents injuries; stretching does not
Source: Lauersen JB, Bertelsen DM, Andersen LB. The effectiveness of exercise interventions to prevent sports injuries: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. Br J Sports Med 2014;48:871-877
Link: https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092538

This meta-analysis of randomised trials found that strength training reduced sports injuries to less than a third, and balance training reduced them by about half. Stretching had no clear effect on injury risk. Stretching can still feel good and help mobility, but it should not replace strength work. Two short strength sessions a week (squats, lunges, calf raises, bridges, planks) are a good base for runners.

## Strength training makes runners faster
Source: Blagrove RC, Howatson G, Hayes PR. Effects of strength training on the physiological determinants of middle- and long-distance running performance: a systematic review. Sports Med 2018;48(5):1117-1149
Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0835-7

This review found that adding strength training (heavy weights and jumping or plyometric exercises) to running improves running economy, meaning the runner uses less energy at the same speed, typically by about 2-8%, and improves time-trial performance. Most studies used 2-3 sessions a week for 6-14 weeks. Strength training did not make runners bulky or slower when done alongside their running.

## Keep most runs easy
Source: Seiler S. What is best practice for training intensity and duration distribution in endurance athletes? Int J Sports Physiol Perform 2010;5(3):276-291
Link: https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.5.3.276

Successful endurance athletes in many sports do about 80% of their training sessions at a low, easy intensity (you can talk in full sentences), and about 20% at hard intensity. Running every session hard leads to tiredness and injury without better results. For most runners this means 1-2 hard sessions a week (intervals, tempo or race), and the rest easy runs, plus a long run at easy pace.

## Bone stress injuries: warning signs
Source: Warden SJ, Davis IS, Fredericson M. Management and prevention of bone stress injuries in long-distance runners. J Orthop Sports Phys Ther 2014;44(10):749-765
Link: https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2014.5334

A bone stress injury (including a stress fracture) builds up when bone is loaded faster than it can repair. Warning signs are pain in one spot on a bone (for example the shin, foot or hip) that gets worse with running, tenderness when you press that spot, and later pain while walking or at night. Risk is higher with sudden increases in training, past bone stress injury, under-fuelling, low calcium or vitamin D, and in female runners with missed periods. Stop running and see a doctor; it usually needs rest from running and a scan.

## Achilles tendon pain: load it, do not just rest it
Source: Alfredson H, Pietila T, Jonsson P, Lorentzon R. Heavy-load eccentric calf muscle training for the treatment of chronic Achilles tendinosis. Am J Sports Med 1998;26(3):360-366
Link: https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465980260030301

Long-lasting Achilles tendon pain in runners usually improves with slow, heavy calf exercise rather than complete rest. In this classic study, runners did slow heel drops off a step (3 sets of 15 with the knee straight and 3 sets of 15 with the knee bent, twice a day for 12 weeks), and all returned to running. Mild discomfort during the exercise was accepted. This should be done with a physiotherapist's guidance; sudden sharp pain, a snap or swelling needs a doctor.
