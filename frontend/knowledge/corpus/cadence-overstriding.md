## What overstriding means
Source: Heiderscheit BC et al. Effects of step rate manipulation on joint mechanics during running. Med Sci Sports Exerc 2011;43(2):296-302
Link: https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181ebedf4

Overstriding means the foot lands far in front of the body, with the knee nearly straight. This acts like a brake at every step and sends more load into the knee and hip. In the app's form check this shows up as "Contact placement": the foot should land closer to under the hips, with the knee slightly bent. Taking slightly shorter, quicker steps is the simplest way to bring the landing point back under the body.

## Raising step rate by 5-10% reduces load on knees and hips
Source: Heiderscheit BC et al. Effects of step rate manipulation on joint mechanics during running. Med Sci Sports Exerc 2011;43(2):296-302
Link: https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181ebedf4

In a lab study of 45 recreational runners, raising step rate (steps per minute) by 5% at the same speed reduced the energy the knee had to absorb, and raising it by 10% reduced the load at both the knee and the hip. With more steps, the body bounced up and down less, braking was smaller and the foot landed closer to the body. A small increase of 5-10% from your own natural cadence is enough; big jumps are not needed.

## Higher step rate and runner's knee pain
Source: Lenhart RL, Thelen DG, Wille CM, Chumanov ES, Heiderscheit BC. Increasing running step rate reduces patellofemoral joint forces. Med Sci Sports Exerc 2014;46(3):557-564
Link: https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3182a78c3a

Pain around or behind the kneecap (patellofemoral pain, "runner's knee") is one of the most common running injuries. This study showed that raising step rate by 10% lowered the peak force on the kneecap joint during running. That is why raising cadence slightly is one of the first form changes physios use for runner's knee, together with strengthening the hips and thighs and adjusting training load.

## How to raise your cadence
Source: Willy RW et al. In-field gait retraining and mobile monitoring to address running biomechanics associated with tibial stress fracture. Scand J Med Sci Sports 2016;26(2):197-205
Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=In-field+gait+retraining+and+mobile+monitoring+to+address+running+biomechanics+associated+with+tibial+stress+fracture

In this study, runners raised their step rate by about 7.5% using simple feedback during their normal outdoor runs, and the lower impact loading was still there a month later. To do it yourself: count your steps for 30 seconds on an easy run and double it to get steps per minute. Set a metronome app 5-7% higher and run with it for a few minutes at a time, several times in a run. Keep the same speed and simply take quicker, shorter steps. After a few weeks the new rhythm feels natural.

## There is no perfect cadence of 180
Source: Heiderscheit BC et al. Effects of step rate manipulation on joint mechanics during running. Med Sci Sports Exerc 2011;43(2):296-302
Link: https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181ebedf4

The famous "180 steps per minute" comes from watching elite runners at race pace; it is not a target for everyone. Natural cadence changes with speed, height and leg length, and it rises as you run faster. Research supports small changes from your own baseline (about 5-10%) rather than forcing a fixed number. If you have no pain and no clear overstride, you do not need to change your cadence.
