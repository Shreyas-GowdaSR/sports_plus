---
title: Foot strike: heel, midfoot or forefoot
topic: form
---

## Most distance runners land on the heel
Source: Larson P et al. Foot strike patterns of recreational and sub-elite runners in a long-distance road race. J Sports Sci 2011;29(15):1665-1673
Link: https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2011.610347

When runners were filmed in a marathon, about 89% landed heel-first at the 10 km point and about 93% by the 32 km point, as they got tired. Even many fast runners land on the heel. Heel striking is therefore the normal pattern for distance runners and is not, by itself, a mistake.

## No foot strike is best for everyone
Source: Hamill J, Gruber AH. Is changing footstrike pattern beneficial to runners? J Sport Health Sci 2017;6(2):146-153
Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jshs.2017.02.004

This review found no strong evidence that changing from a heel strike to a midfoot or forefoot strike prevents injuries or makes running more economical for most runners. Changing foot strike moves the load rather than removing it: a forefoot strike puts less load on the knee but more on the ankle, calf and Achilles tendon. Where the foot lands relative to the body (overstriding) matters more than which part of the foot touches first.

## Heel striking and injury in one college team
Source: Daoud AI et al. Foot strike and injury rates in endurance runners: a retrospective study. Med Sci Sports Exerc 2012;44(7):1325-1334
Link: https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3182465115

In a Harvard college cross-country team, runners who mostly landed on the heel reported about twice the rate of repetitive stress injuries compared with those who mostly landed on the forefoot. This was a small, backward-looking study in one team, so it shows a link, not proof that heel striking causes injury. It is one reason some coaches suggest a softer, flatter landing for runners with repeated knee or shin problems.

## How to change foot strike safely
Source: Barton CJ et al. Running retraining to treat lower limb injuries: a mixed-methods study of current evidence synthesised with expert opinion. Br J Sports Med 2016;50:513-526
Link: https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-095278

Experts in this review use foot-strike change for some specific injuries, not for every runner. If you change towards a midfoot landing, do it slowly: start with short sections (for example 1 minute in every 5) on easy runs, add calf raises to strengthen the calf and Achilles, and expect some calf soreness at first. Stop and get advice if you get Achilles or foot pain. Often simply taking shorter, quicker steps makes the landing flatter by itself.
