## Food first: which supplements have real evidence
Source: Maughan RJ et al. IOC consensus statement: dietary supplements and the high-performance athlete. Br J Sports Med 2018;52:439-455
Link: https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2018-099027

The IOC consensus statement says a good diet comes first, and very few supplements improve performance. Those with good evidence in some situations are caffeine, creatine, dietary nitrate (beetroot juice), beta-alanine and sodium bicarbonate. Each works only for certain kinds of events and has side effects for some people. Supplements such as iron or vitamin D are useful only when a blood test shows a deficiency. Young athletes in particular should focus on food, training and sleep, and use supplements only with advice from a sports doctor or sports dietitian.

## Contaminated supplements and failed dope tests
Source: Maughan RJ et al. IOC consensus statement: dietary supplements and the high-performance athlete. Br J Sports Med 2018;52:439-455
Link: https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2018-099027

Some supplements, including protein powders, "fat burners" and "muscle gainers", are contaminated with banned substances such as steroids or stimulants that are not listed on the label. Under anti-doping rules (WADA, and NADA in India) athletes are strictly responsible for anything found in their sample, so a contaminated product can cause a ban. Athletes who are tested should avoid supplements unless needed, use batch-tested products, and check with a sports doctor.

## Caffeine from coffee and chai before training
Source: Guest NS et al. International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand: caffeine and exercise performance. J Int Soc Sports Nutr 2021;18:1
Link: https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-020-00383-4

Caffeine can make endurance exercise feel easier and improve performance. The ISSN position stand says benefits are seen with moderate amounts taken about 60 minutes before exercise, and that lower amounts can also help. People respond very differently: some get a racing heart, anxiety, stomach upset or poor sleep. Everyday sources are filter coffee, instant coffee and chai (chai has less caffeine than coffee). Avoid caffeine late in the day because it harms sleep, try it in training before using it in a race, and children and teenagers should avoid caffeine supplements and energy drinks.
