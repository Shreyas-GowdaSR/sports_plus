## Getting used to running in the heat (acclimatisation)
Source: Racinais S et al. Consensus recommendations on training and competing in the heat. Br J Sports Med 2015;49:1164-1173
Link: https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-094915

The body adapts to heat with repeated training in hot conditions: sweating starts earlier, heart rate is lower and the body stays cooler. Most of the change happens in the first week and it is largely complete in about two weeks, with daily sessions of about 60-90 minutes in the heat. During that time, reduce pace and distance and build up slowly. The adaptation fades over a few weeks without heat exposure. Even when adapted, performance in the heat is lower than in cool weather, so adjust pace targets.

## Planning runs on hot days
Source: Racinais S et al. Consensus recommendations on training and competing in the heat. Br J Sports Med 2015;49:1164-1173
Link: https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-094915

On hot and humid days, run early in the morning or late in the evening, wear light clothing, use shaded routes and carry water or plan water stops. Humidity matters as much as temperature, because sweat cannot evaporate well in humid air. Cold drinks and ice slurries before and during exercise, and pouring cool water on the head and neck, help keep body temperature down. Slow down when the heat index is high, and cancel hard sessions in extreme heat.

## Warning signs of heat illness and first aid
Source: Casa DJ et al. National Athletic Trainers' Association position statement: exertional heat illnesses. J Athl Train 2015;50(9):986-1000
Link: https://doi.org/10.4085/1062-6050-50.9.07

Exertional heat stroke is a medical emergency. Warning signs are confusion, strange behaviour, stumbling, collapse, and a very high body temperature (above 40 degrees C). Other signs of heat illness include headache, dizziness, nausea, chills and goosebumps in the heat. Stop running at once. For suspected heat stroke the rule is "cool first, transport second": cool the whole body as fast as possible, ideally in cold water or with ice water and wet towels, while calling for medical help. Heat stroke treated with fast cooling is almost always survivable.

## Why muscle cramps happen during running
Source: Schwellnus MP et al. Increased running speed and previous cramps rather than dehydration or serum sodium changes predict exercise-associated muscle cramping: a prospective cohort study in 210 Ironman triathletes. Br J Sports Med 2011;45:650-656
Link: https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.2010.078535

Cramps during exercise are often blamed on dehydration or low salt, but research does not strongly support this. In a study of 210 Ironman triathletes, those who cramped were not more dehydrated and did not have lower blood salt. Cramping was linked to racing faster than usual (relative to training) and to a history of cramps. Muscle fatigue is the most likely main cause. Practical steps: pace yourself sensibly, train at race pace so the muscles are ready, and stretch the cramping muscle gently when it happens. Salt and fluid still matter for long hot runs, but they are not a guaranteed cure.
