## How much protein an athlete needs in a day
Source: Jager R et al. International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand: protein and exercise. J Int Soc Sports Nutr 2017;14:20
Link: https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8

For most people who exercise, 1.4-2.0 g of protein per kg of body weight per day is enough to build and keep muscle. The ACSM joint position stand gives a similar range of 1.2-2.0 g per kg. Endurance runners usually need the middle of the range. People doing heavy strength training, or trying to keep muscle while eating less, need the top end. Getting enough protein across the whole day matters more than any single meal or the exact timing.

## Athletes need more protein than the general Indian RDA
Source: ICMR-National Institute of Nutrition. Nutrient Requirements for Indians: Recommended Dietary Allowances and Estimated Average Requirements. Hyderabad, 2020
Link: https://www.nin.res.in/
Cite: ICMR-NIN 2020

The Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for protein for healthy Indian adults is about 0.83 g per kg of body weight per day (ICMR-NIN 2020). This is meant for people who do not train hard. Athletes in regular training need clearly more, about 1.4-2.0 g per kg per day. Many Indian vegetarian diets built mainly on rice or wheat fall short of this unless dal, milk, curd, paneer, soya, eggs or meat are added at most meals.

## How much protein per meal and how often
Source: Schoenfeld BJ, Aragon AA. How much protein can the body use in a single meal for muscle-building? J Int Soc Sports Nutr 2018;15:10
Link: https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-018-0215-1

Muscle repair works best when protein is spread across the day, not eaten in one big meal. The ISSN advises about 0.25 g per kg (roughly 20-40 g) per meal, every 3-4 hours. For people who want to build muscle, Schoenfeld and Aragon suggest about 0.4 g per kg per meal across at least four meals, which adds up to about 1.6 g per kg per day. In practice: include a protein food in breakfast, lunch, the evening snack and dinner.

## Protein after training
Source: Thomas DT, Erdman KA, Burke LM. ACSM, AND and Dietitians of Canada joint position: Nutrition and Athletic Performance. Med Sci Sports Exerc 2016;48(3):543-568
Link: https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000852

About 0.3 g protein per kg of body weight soon after a key session (within about 0 to 2 hours), and again every 3-5 hours, supports muscle repair. After a run, pair the protein with carbohydrate, for example curd rice, milk with banana, egg with chapati, or dal rice. The idea of a strict 30-minute "window" is overstated: if a meal with protein was eaten 1-2 hours before training, the next meal can come a little later without harm.

## Protein before sleep
Source: Jager R et al. International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand: protein and exercise. J Int Soc Sports Nutr 2017;14:20
Link: https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8

Eating about 30-40 g of slowly digested protein (casein, which is the main protein in milk) 30 minutes or so before sleep can increase overnight muscle repair. For an Indian athlete, a glass of milk with dinner or before bed, curd, or paneer at dinner does a similar job using normal food.

## Protein for vegetarian and vegan athletes
Source: Rogerson D. Vegan diets: practical advice for athletes and exercisers. J Int Soc Sports Nutr 2017;14:36
Link: https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0192-9

Plant proteins usually have less of some essential amino acids (lysine in cereals, methionine in pulses) and are digested a little less well. Vegetarian and vegan athletes can still meet their needs by eating a mix of protein foods through the day (cereal plus pulse, as in dal rice, rajma chawal, idli sambar, chapati with chana) and by aiming for the higher end of the protein range. Good Indian plant sources are soya chunks, tofu, chana, rajma, moong and other dals, peanuts and seeds. Vegans should also plan for vitamin B12, iron, calcium, zinc and iodine.

## Protein powders: ICMR advice and contamination risk
Source: Maughan RJ et al. IOC consensus statement: dietary supplements and the high-performance athlete. Br J Sports Med 2018;52:439-455
Link: https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2018-099027

The ICMR-NIN Dietary Guidelines for Indians (2024) advise against using protein supplements to build body mass, and recommend getting protein from food. The IOC consensus statement on supplements says protein powders can be convenient but are not needed when food intake is enough. It also warns that supplements, including protein powders, are sometimes contaminated with banned substances. For a competitive athlete this can cause a failed dope test, because the athlete is responsible for anything found in their body. Food first: milk, curd, paneer, eggs, dal, soya, chicken or fish.
